01 · Identity

One person, a fresh identity for every relationship.

From one seed on their devices, a person derives a new identifier for each contact, group and public appearance. No one else can link them. The relationships know the person. The network does not.

Real Life Trust Protocol

Identity follows the relationship, not the other way round. One seed, kept by the person, derives an anchor per context: this contact, that group, the public. Nobody who sees two of them can tell they are the same person. This we share with the wider ecosystem (personas per context); the difference is where the seed lives and who derives: the person's own devices, not an agent minting on their behalf. Rotation and recovery follow from the seed, so a lost device does not mean a lost person. The picture starts where a person starts: twelve magic words (English BIP-39), which become the seed. Each anchor is a did:key, self-certifying, no registry. The last step gathers every anchor into one document, the person's own encrypted state: which contexts exist and under which anchor. The follow-on question: where does that document live? Next slide.

Compared with OpenVTC

The idea is shared: one persona per context. In OpenVTC a persona exists per community, minted by the agent as a did:webvh, which needs a DID host. Here it exists per relationship, derived on the device from one seed, with no hosting.

02 · Person

The person is the node.

A person's keys live on their own devices, and each device can act on its own. Nothing has to be running for two people to meet or decide. An agent may help. It is never a condition.

Real Life Trust Protocol

Where does a person's ability to act live? Here: with the person, on their devices, each holding its own keys. In the ecosystem picture every node has a trust agent that holds the keys and signs for the person; the devices are clients of it. Here an agent or a relay may help with reachability and delivery, but nothing has to be running for two people to meet, sign or decide. The honest price: loss and recovery rest with the person, and being reachable between sessions needs a carrier. Open: restoring a person onto a new device is not built yet. An agent can also be a contact of its own, with its own keys and relationships; no key reveals whether a person or an agent holds it. Open: whether one can tell that one is meeting an agent. The bridge from the previous slide: the magic words restore every key, but which contexts exist is data in the person's encrypted state, the personal document every device holds and keeps in step (Identity 6.3); recovery needs both. A relay may carry that document between devices, sealed; it never reads it.

Compared with OpenVTC

In OpenVTC the node is a trust agent (VTA), a signing oracle with a policy: the master seed lives in the VTA, clients send unsigned data and the VTA signs inside its own process. Hosting is recommended, self-hosting possible, and the DTG spec also allows local agents. Here the devices hold the keys and act themselves. We have not seen an encounter or joining flow there without an agent; a browser tab joining a data room without an agent has been shown.

03 · Relationship

Relationships between people are the root of all trust.

Trust starts between two people who met. Groups grow from these relationships, under rules of their own. Nothing else is required for it.

Real Life Trust Protocol

The thesis of the deck. In ToIP terms, the relationship is our spanning layer: everything else, groups, hosts, agents, is built on it and may never be required for it. The picture: first the links between pairs, then, where they grow dense, a group becomes visible. One person is in both groups; two people at the edge are in none and still connected. No registry, resolver, platform or issuer has to make a relationship valid. The honest price: reach. What two people know, no stranger learns by default; trust across strangers has to be built on purpose. Open: how far standing may travel beyond the relationship.

Compared with OpenVTC

In OpenVTC the basic unit is the community (VTC), with its own DID, member list, policy engine and registry. Relationship credentials between people exist, but as an edge in the graph they count for a verifier only if the issuer's community membership is proven. There the relationship is bound to the community; here the community is bound to the relationships.

04 · Trust

Trust is not a level. It is what you choose to show.

After an encounter, a contact sees the stars: who we both know and which groups we share, blinded. The Trust act gives that one contact the key that ties my relationships together, and with it who I am in our groups, my name and picture, and that they may introduce me. Nothing carries a degree. A class answers what, not how much.

Real Life Trust Protocol

Trust Layer 0.31, section 2a (branch trust/0.31; 0.32 adds the introduction class). Five classes today: the star (which of my contacts this contact also knows, blinded, verifiable by the addressee alone and only for anchors it holds), the blinded group star (which groups we share), the anchor mapping (the key that ties my relationships together; off toward everyone until the Trust act), the sealed group star (my member anchor in a group we share), the profile (name, picture). Each stands per contact on default, on or off; the Trust act turns the anchor mapping on, and every class whose default is "trusted contacts" follows. Taking the mapping back stops what followed the default; what was switched on by hand stays on. On the slide, three steps: first the panel as Ben sees it after the encounter (the two stars are on by default toward every contact); then the Trust button; then the same panel after the Trust act, the key on and every class with default "trusted contacts" following. Rule 2750: no artifact of a trust class may carry a field that expresses a degree of trust. A global score is either forgeable or central. Rule 1010: an anchor reaches a party only through an act of its issuer toward that party; nobody forwards another person's key.

Compared with OpenVTC

In OpenVTC trust in a person runs through the community: roles (admin, moderator, issuer, member), policy in Rego, endorsements. A trust act between two people that opens the link between one's own identities to that one contact alone has no counterpart there. Access is granted per community and role there, per contact and class here. OpenVTC does not assign a score either.

05 · Audience

Knowing is not proving.

What you learn about my relationships, you cannot pass on as proof. Before anything gets a proof, two questions: whom must it convince, and must the receiver be able to show it on? Most things only need to convince one person, so only that person can check them. Cards and encounter credentials are signed for anyone. What ties two of my identities together is never signed. What I say about others is blinded.

Real Life Trust Protocol

Trust Layer 0.31, section 3, the audience principle. Two questions decide every artifact's cryptographic form: F1, whom must it convince for its purpose; F2, must the recipient be able to pass it on. The verifier set must be the smallest the purpose demands (3010); the deniable form is the default and a transferable signature carries the burden of justification (3020). Four audience classes: S, about myself and addressed to one (profile, anchor mapping): MAC, only the addressee. P, presentable (self card, encounter credential, membership document): signed for anyone (3030). V, links between two contexts of one person (continuity mapping, group pair, introduction voucher): deniable, and a cross-context link must not exist anywhere as a transferable proof (3040). D, statements about third parties (star, group star, continuity probe): deniable and blinded, because the knowledge itself harms (3060). Central commitment of the spec: links between anchors are the protected good. This is our answer to trust that travels: it travels with the person, through cards and introductions, never as a transferable proof about a relationship.

Compared with OpenVTC

In OpenVTC the presentable form is the norm: credentials with proofs, status lists that outside verifiers read without a login, a registry with a query protocol. Zero-knowledge presentation is meant to become the default but is switched off behind a feature flag. Here the deniable form is the norm, and a transferable signature is the exception that needs a reason.

06 · Relational reading

Trust is read from where you stand.

Identities are free, so a swarm can vouch for itself. That is why "many people know me" counts only like this: at least N, of which k inside the trust of whoever is asking. Ben reads Ana's relationships differently from Sam, because they trust different people. There is no number everyone reads the same way. It would be forgeable, or central.

Real Life Trust Protocol

Trust Layer 0.31, section 7, relational counts, and the Personhood Predicates spec (verifier-relative witnessing predicates). Anchors are free; a Sybil swarm issues itself arbitrary self-evidence. The scarce resource is the boundary edge into the honest graph, and the blinded star is the verifier-side instrument: it tells the reader which of Ana's contacts they already hold themselves. Rule 7010: a count or personhood statement must be anchored relationally, at least N of which k inside the verifier's trust horizon, never absolutely. Rule 7020: no consuming layer may accept a global per-everyone score. Honest: the spec calls this a normative principle whose proof artifact is unwritten (7030); until then it binds as a design constraint. This is what carries the sentence on slide 04: being a person is what your encounters add up to, read from where the reader stands.

Compared with OpenVTC

In OpenVTC personhood is a flag on the membership credential, by its own documentation a non-binding hint and a governance decision of the community; the registry says who is a member. Here personhood is read by each viewer from their own horizon. Shared by both: neither accepts a global number.

07 · Invitation

Invitation, not checkpoint.

Nobody has to present anything to get in, not at an encounter and not into a group. Those who let you in know you. The devices only check that the invitation followed the group's rules and that you said yes yourself. No credential is produced that you would have to show somewhere else.

Real Life Trust Protocol

The admission question, before the group itself. A checkpoint is where a stranger decides on the strength of a proof you can show anywhere, usually with a third party keeping the log; that has its place where strangers meet, at borders. Inside a community nobody needs it: the person who invites you knows you. Joining a group gives you a fresh key for that group that only the inviter knows; there is nothing to present. The members' devices check three things: was the inviter allowed to invite under the group's rules, did enough members agree, did the guest say yes themselves (Membership Tasks 1.2, Access 5.3). The checks run on the devices of those involved, and no proof to carry elsewhere is produced. First in the picture: show, check, admit, and a log that grows with every check. Then, in its place: know, invite, share. The honest price: someone who knows nobody cannot get in; access for strangers is something a group has to build on purpose, and that is where presentable cards (slide 06) come in. In their words: membranes and gatekeeping for communities; here the membrane is the relationships themselves.

Compared with OpenVTC

In OpenVTC joining is a checkpoint: a policy (default: deny all) plus admin approval, even for open requests; invitation credentials replace the waiting, not the check. A target policy of "two vouchers from the membership" is written down but not active. Here members invite, and the devices only check the rule.

08 · Group

No one stands above the group’s own record.

A group is a signed record every member holds, and its rules are part of it: who may invite, who may remove, how the rules change. People join by invitation and their own consent (slide 08). No role stands above the record. When the founder leaves, the group goes on.

Real Life Trust Protocol

Who holds the truth about a group, and what happens when they are gone? Here the truth is one record, held by every member; there is no single point that keeps it. The rules are data in the record: who may invite, who may remove, with what threshold or vouching, how the rules themselves change. Joining takes an invitation and the person's own signed consent. The founder is a member like any other; the picture shows Ana leaving and the group admitting Lea afterwards. A group may still give one person the say, like a company does; the difference to a platform admin is that this person is a member inside the record, not a party outside it. Elsewhere this seat is an owner, a steward or an admin. In their words: groups that own themselves, a single point of truth for membership, forty years of memory. The honest price: complexity. Concurrent changes need fixed rules for every pairing, and there is no kill switch when a group goes wrong. Shown: the authority log and membership tasks are specified and merged; experiments cover groups without an owner and removal that rotates the keys. Open: conflict resolution among people, graduated sanctions and mediation (Ostrom 5 and 6). The record keeps decisions; it does not make them.

Compared with OpenVTC

In OpenVTC a community is a server with its own database and cached signing keys; its internal access list is authoritative, and M-of-N governance is designed but not built. Data rooms have one owner by invariant: controller of the room DID, issuer of all its credentials, sole MLS committer. Here every member holds the record, and the rules are data inside it.

09 · Offline

A neighborhood keeps deciding when the network is gone.

The group lives on its members' devices, and every copy may change. Two people who meet can carry on alone; what they decided apart is merged by the group's own rules when the others return. Relays, mesh or a phone next to yours only carry sealed bytes. No host has to be awake.

Real Life Trust Protocol

In their words: is web access necessary and sufficient? Here: neither. The group lives on its members' devices; there is no host that could be missing. Every copy may change, and the rules of the previous slide decide what concurrent changes mean, so two people who meet can carry on alone and the rest merges when the others return. Carriers (a relay, a mesh, a phone next to yours) only move sealed bytes and are interchangeable. The protocol asks a carrier for one small promise: a message arrives eventually and is never silently lost; how long that takes is open, a carrier pigeon qualifies. A carrier sees an address per relationship, never a person, so a person’s relationships do not converge at any service (Delivery 5a.2). The honest price: conflicts become visible instead of prevented; two truths may stand side by side for a while. Shown: replication with CRDTs in the app, the replication rules in the spec, delivery through an independent mediator. Honest too: a real run without any network, between neighbours' devices, has barely happened. Open: exactly that run, with an offline box or a mesh among neighbours. The question for the room: for which communities is the hour without a host an edge case, and for which is it daily life?

Compared with OpenVTC

OpenVTC has a mediator with store-and-forward, so the recipient need not be online at the same time; but agent, DID host and mediator must be reachable. Data rooms explicitly reject CRDTs in favour of one write primary with version preconditions. Concurrent offline writing is deliberately not a goal there and the normal case here. Honestly, our own run with no network at all has barely happened yet.

10 · On the wire

On the wire, we speak the ecosystem's forms.

The principles are ours. The forms are shared: DTG credentials, Trust Task documents, TSP through a mediator, did:key identities. A second implementation can read what we send, and we can read theirs. That is what a build weekend can test.

Real Life Trust Protocol

The evidence slide. Encounter credential: W3C VC 2.0 with the DTG context as second context (Encounter 0.31, rule 2260), pinned at WD01; shared test vectors with Consensual, confirmed by their independent verifier. Invitation: a conformant DTG InvitationCredential inside membership-invite 0.2 (Membership Tasks 0.16+). Messages: Trust Task documents under a private namespace, target framework 0.4; the registry is at 0.6 with 0.7 in draft, and the gap (URIs without /spec/, MAC acks, dedup by id) is listed and open. Transport: TSP Rev 3, routed, through the VTI mediator, measured live 12 of 12 (E11b, merged 21 Oct). Identities: did:key from one seed, pairwise anchors. Not used: r-cards (JSContact) for profiles; an idea for interop, not built. Browser tab writing into an OpenVTC data room without an agent: shown 22 Oct, a storage-level proof rather than a trust-level one.

Compared with OpenVTC

This is where we meet OpenVTC: DTG credentials, Trust Tasks, TSP through the same mediator, did:key. Still open are the gap between the Trust Task framework versions and whether our encounter credentials count as edges in its graph.

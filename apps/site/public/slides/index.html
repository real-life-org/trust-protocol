What we build

Trust infrastructure for self-organizing communities.

A protocol for people who meet, and for the groups they form. Nothing has to be hosted for two people to connect, nobody issues who you are, and nobody judges on your behalf. The ten principles that follow say how.

Real Life Trust Protocol

  • Three things in one breath, before the principles.
  • Two phones, no uplink: a meeting leaves two credentials, one from each side. That is the whole root.
  • No operator in the trust path: it works local-first and offline; relays and agents may help, they are never in between.
  • No inherited verdict: nobody hands you a score. Every reader judges from where they stand.
  • The next ten slides say how, one principle each.

VTI

  • The node is a trust agent (VTA): it holds the keys, and without one a person cannot be represented.
  • A community node decides admission and answers status queries live.
  • Verdicts are issued and travel: membership, the personhood flag, endorsements.

RLTP

  • The devices hold the keys; nothing has to be hosted.
  • Every reader judges from where they stand; no verdict is issued.

01 · Identity

One person, a fresh identity for every relationship.

From one seed on their devices, a person derives a new identifier for each contact, group and public appearance. No one else can link them, unless the person chooses to. The relationships know the person. The network does not.

Real Life Trust Protocol

  • A person starts with twelve words. They become one seed.
  • From that seed the phone derives a fresh identifier for every context: this contact, that group, the public.
  • Nobody who sees two of them can tell they belong to the same person. Only the person can reveal that, and that is the Trust act later.
  • Where we agree with the DTG: one identifier per counterparty. Where we differ: the DTG’s agent derives the identifiers; here the phone does.
  • The last step gathers every identifier into one document, the person’s own. Next: where does it live?
  • Sources: Identity 0.55 §5–6; DTG Credentials WD 0.6.0 issuerScope; VTI WD 0.2.0 key derivation

VTI

  • A persona per community, minted by the agent as did:webvh, which needs a DID host.

RLTP

  • An anchor per relationship, derived on the device from one seed, no hosting.

Shared

  • One persona per context.

02 · Person

The person is the node.

A person's keys live on their own devices, and each device can act on its own. Nothing has to be running for two people to meet or decide. An agent may help. It is never a condition.

Real Life Trust Protocol

  • That document lives on the person’s devices. Each device holds the keys and can act alone.
  • Nothing has to be running for two people to meet, sign or decide.
  • A storage service may keep an encrypted copy, for recovery. A relay may carry it. Neither can read it.
  • In VTI a person without an agent cannot be represented at all. Here an agent may help; it is never a condition.
  • Honest: restoring a person onto a new device is specified, not built yet.
  • Sources: Identity 0.55 §5.5, §6.3, Status; VTI WD 0.2.0 architecture; FPP 1.2

VTI

  • The node is a trust agent (VTA): it holds the seed and signs for the person; clients send it unsigned data. It may run on the person’s device or on a host.

RLTP

  • The devices hold the keys and act themselves; an agent may help, never a condition.

03 · Relationship

Relationships between people are the root of all trust.

Trust starts between two people who met. Groups grow from these relationships, under rules of their own. Nothing else is required for it.

Real Life Trust Protocol

  • This is the thesis. Trust starts between two people who met.
  • Ana and Ben scan each other. Each phone issues one credential about the other, with a fresh challenge. Both keep both.
  • Nobody issued it, so nobody can revoke it, expire it or turn it into a score.
  • It proves only that the key was in the right hands and a human decided. Not that they stood in the same room, not who they are.
  • The network grows from such meetings, friend by friend, without any group yet. Groups come later.
  • Where we agree with the DTG: two credentials, one per direction. Where we differ: the DTG anchors personhood in a community; here it is read later, from encounters.
  • Sources: Encounter 0.32 §3, §4, §7, rule 8020; DTG Credentials WD 0.6.0 edges; ToIP TAS PR02

VTI

  • The basic unit is the community (VTC): own DID, member list, policy engine, registry. A relationship credential counts as an edge only if the issuer’s membership is proven.
  • Membership, the personhood flag and endorsements are issued by the community, carry a status and expire.

RLTP

  • The relationship is the unit; communities are bound to relationships, not the other way round.
  • No issuer, no status endpoint, no renewal.

Shared

  • Relationship credentials in pairs, one per direction, and the in-person check with a spoken code.

04 · Trust

Trust is not a level. It is what you choose to show.

After an encounter, a contact sees the stars: who we both know and which groups we share, blinded. The Trust act gives that one contact the key that ties my relationships together, and with it who I am in our groups, my name and picture, and that they may introduce me. Nothing carries a degree. A class answers what, not how much.

Real Life Trust Protocol

  • After a meeting, a contact sees two things: who we both know, and which groups we share. Both blinded.
  • Trust is a second act. One button, toward one contact.
  • It hands that contact the one key that ties my relationships together. Nothing else changes, nothing travels further.
  • There are no levels. What I show you is the measure of my trust in you.
  • No artifact carries a score. A global score would be forgeable or central.
  • The introduction row is an outlook: not in the published 0.31, drafted for 0.32.
  • Sources: Trust Layer 0.31 §2a, rules 2720, 2750, 2770, 1010; DTG endorsements; FPP 1.2 vouching

VTI

  • Trust in a person runs through the community: roles (admin, moderator, issuer, member), policy in Rego, endorsements as statement credentials.

RLTP

  • One act toward one contact that opens the link between my own identities to that contact alone; access per contact and class.

05 · Audience

Knowing is not proving.

Before anything gets a proof, two questions: whom must it convince, and may the receiver show it on? Most things only need to convince one person, so only that person can check them. Cards and encounter credentials are signed, because their reader is someone I have not met. What ties two of my identities together is never signed. What I say about others is blinded.

Real Life Trust Protocol

  • Before anything gets a proof, two questions: whom must it convince, and may the receiver show it on?
  • The credential and my card are made to be shown on. Their reader is someone behind you: a group, an introduction. So they are signed.
  • My name and my picture convince you and nobody else. A signature would bind my name to this identity for anyone who ever sees it.
  • “We know someone in common” is blinded. You can only read it against your own contacts; a stranger learns nothing.
  • That two of my identities are one person, you learn after the Trust act. It exists nowhere as a proof. Otherwise anyone holding it could join my relationships up.
  • The DTG does it the other way round: everything signed and transferable, privacy by hiding which credential you show.
  • Sources: Trust Layer 0.31 §3, rules 3010–3060; DTG Credentials WD 0.6.0 privacy considerations

VTI

  • The presentable form is the norm: signed credentials with proofs, status lists and a registry any verifier reads; privacy by hiding which credential is shown (ZKP presentation).

RLTP

  • The deniable form is the norm; a transferable signature is the exception that needs a reason.

06 · Relational reading

Trust is read from where you stand.

Identities are free, so a swarm can vouch for itself. That is why "many people know me" counts only like this: at least N, of which k inside the trust of whoever is asking. Ben reads Ana's relationships differently from Sam, because they trust different people. There is no number everyone reads the same way. It would be forgeable, or central.

Real Life Trust Protocol

  • Identities are free. A swarm of fake ones can vouch for itself all day.
  • So “many people know me” only counts like this: at least N, of which k are inside the trust of whoever is asking.
  • Each person sees only their own star. Laid on top, the shared people and groups light up. Everything else stays dark.
  • Ben reads Ana differently from Sam, because they trust different people. There is no number everyone reads the same way.
  • The DTG agrees that reading is verifier-relative; its anchor is an accredited community. Ours is the reader’s own contacts.
  • Honest: the proof artifact for these counts is not written yet; today it is a design rule.
  • Sources: Trust Layer 0.31 §7, rules 7010–7030; Personhood Predicates 0.12; DTG Credentials WD 0.6.0 edge verifiability

VTI

  • Personhood is a flag on the membership credential, by its own documentation a non-binding hint and a governance decision; the registry says who is a member.

RLTP

  • Personhood is read by each viewer from their own horizon.

07 · Invitation

Invitation, not checkpoint.

Nobody has to present anything to get in, not at an encounter and not into a group. Those who let you in know you. The devices only check that the invitation followed the group's rules and that you said yes yourself. No credential is produced that you would have to show somewhere else.

Real Life Trust Protocol

  • How do you get in? Not by presenting something at a door.
  • A checkpoint: a stranger decides on a proof you can show anywhere, and a third party keeps a log. It has its place, at borders.
  • Inside a community nobody needs it. The person who invites you knows you.
  • Your phone and the members’ phones check three things: was the inviter allowed to invite, did enough members agree, did you say yes yourself.
  • You get a fresh key for the group. Members see that key; only the inviter can tie it to the person they know.
  • Nothing portable comes out of it. The DTG also invites and asks consent; the difference is that there a community node decides, here the devices check the rule.
  • Honest price: someone who knows nobody cannot get in. Access for strangers is something a group builds on purpose.
  • Sources: Membership Tasks 0.21.1 admission; Access 0.58 §5.3; VTI WD 0.2.0 MEM-011/020; DTG Invitation Credential

VTI

  • Admission is a decision of the community node, under policies written in Rego: who may vet, who may vouch, what counts.
  • Vetting and membership come back as credentials the community issues (VSC, VMC); the node records who is in good standing.

RLTP

  • Members invite; the devices of those involved check the group’s own rule and the invitee’s signed yes.
  • No node decides, no log is kept, nothing portable comes out of it.

Shared

  • Invitation plus the member’s own consent, on both sides.

08 · Group

No one stands above the group’s own record.

A group is a signed record every member holds, and its rules are part of it: who may invite, who may remove, how the rules change. People join by invitation and their own consent (slide 08). No role stands above the record. When the founder leaves, the group goes on.

Real Life Trust Protocol

  • Who holds the truth about a group? Every member, as one signed record.
  • The rules are part of the record: who may invite, who may remove, how the rules change.
  • No role stands above it. A founder is a member like any other; here Ana leaves, and the group admits Lea afterwards.
  • A group may still give one person the say. The difference to an admin is that this person is inside the record, not a party outside it.
  • When members disagree, the record does not pick a winner. The group stops, visibly, until a quorum of both sides reconciles.
  • Honest: the rules run in an experimental harness today, not yet in the library.
  • Sources: Access 0.58 §3, §3.6, Status; VTI WD 0.2.0 VTC-001; FPP 1.2 communities

VTI

  • The community is a node on a trust agent; its record of who belongs is authoritative, and governance is policy the node enforces.
  • Data rooms have one owner: controller of the room, issuer of its credentials, sole committer.

RLTP

  • Every member holds the record; the rules are data inside it.

09 · Offline

A neighborhood keeps deciding when the network is gone.

The group lives on its members' devices, and every copy may change. Two people who meet can carry on alone; what they decided apart is merged by the group's own rules when the others return. Relays, mesh or a phone next to yours only carry sealed bytes. No host has to be awake.

Real Life Trust Protocol

  • The group lives on its members’ devices. There is no host that could be missing.
  • Ana writes, Ben writes, apart from each other. When they meet, the group’s own rules merge it.
  • Carriers, a relay, a mesh, a phone next to yours, only move sealed bytes. The protocol asks them for one promise: it arrives eventually, never silently lost.
  • A carrier sees one address per relationship, never a person.
  • Where the VTI mediator agrees with us: it forwards what it cannot read. Where it differs: whether a claim is still true is a live query to the community there; here the copies decide.
  • Honest: the app issues the encounter without a mediator and delivers later; a whole group without any network has not been run yet.
  • Sources: Replication Contract 0.26; Delivery Contract 0.85.2; Identity 0.55 §7a; VTI WD 0.2.0 TRN-020

VTI

  • A mediator stores and forwards, so the recipient need not be online at the same time; agent, DID host and mediator must be reachable.
  • Shared state has one write primary; concurrent offline writing is not a goal.

RLTP

  • Concurrent offline writing is the normal case; the group’s own rules merge it.

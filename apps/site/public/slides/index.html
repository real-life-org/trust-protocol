01 · Identity

One person, a fresh identity for every relationship.

From one seed on their devices, a person derives a new identifier for each contact, group and public appearance. No one else can link them, unless the person chooses to. The relationships know the person. The network does not.

Real Life Trust Protocol

  • Shared ground: DTG Credentials WD 0.6.0 declares a correlation scope per identifier (issuerScope pairwise, directed, public); pairwise is RECOMMENDED, and a pairwise identifier “MUST NOT be used with more than one counterparty”. Our encounter credentials declare exactly that.
  • Here (Identity 0.55): twelve magic words (English BIP-39) become the seed; from it one anchor per context, this contact, that group, the public. Each anchor is a did:key, self-certifying, no registry, no resolver.
  • Without the seed, two anchors of one person are unlinkable; linking them takes the person’s own act of disclosure (the Trust act, slide 04).
  • The fork: in VTI the agent “owns the trust context tree and key derivation within it”; here the seed and every derivation stay on the person’s devices.
  • Encounter 0.32 goes further than one identifier per relationship: a fresh pairwise identifier per enactment; a blinded continuity probe (Trust Layer 6a) recognises the same person twice without a stable identifier travelling.
  • Rotation of the community anchor is bound in advance (pre-rotation, Identity 5.4).
  • Last step of the picture: every anchor in the person’s own document. Next slide: where that document lives.

Compared with OpenVTC

  • Shared: one persona per context.
  • There: a persona per community, minted by the agent as did:webvh, which needs a DID host.
  • Here: an anchor per relationship, derived on the device from one seed, no hosting.

02 · Person

The person is the node.

A person's keys live on their own devices, and each device can act on its own. Nothing has to be running for two people to meet or decide. An agent may help. It is never a condition.

Real Life Trust Protocol

  • VTI WD 0.2.0: “A VTA is the root of everything else … A principal with no VTA has no way to be represented”; clients act “under granted authority”. First Person v1.2: VTA keys in the cloud are controlled by keys at the edge. The code moves ahead of the spec: since October a browser tab joins a data room without a VTA (vti-rooms-wasm).
  • Here: every device of the person holds the root key material and derives its own device key per context (Identity 5.5). Nothing has to be running for two people to meet, sign or decide.
  • A storage service holds the person’s encrypted state (Identity 6.3): sealed, needed for recovery, not for acting. A relay may carry it between devices; it never reads it. The magic words restore the keys; which contexts exist is data in that document; recovery needs both.
  • An agent can be a contact of its own, with its own keys and relationships; no key reveals whether a person or an agent holds it.
  • Price: loss and recovery rest with the person; being reachable between sessions needs a carrier.
  • Status (Identity, Status of This Document): the recovery context is not implemented; one writer per holder stands in for the coordination of several devices.

Compared with OpenVTC

  • There: the node is a trust agent (VTA), a signing oracle with a policy; the seed lives in the agent, clients send unsigned data. Hosting recommended, self-hosting possible.
  • Here: the devices hold the keys and act themselves; an agent may help, never a condition.
  • Code moves ahead of the spec: a browser tab joins a data room without an agent (October).

03 · Relationship

Relationships between people are the root of all trust.

Trust starts between two people who met. Groups grow from these relationships, under rules of their own. Nothing else is required for it.

Real Life Trust Protocol

  • The thesis. ToIP TAS PR02 calls Layer 2 “the trust spanning layer”; First Person v1.2 calls TSP “the keystone”. Here the spanning layer is the relationship between two people who met; groups, carriers and agents are built on it and never required for it (README: Delivery and Replication are “services behind ports, not layers”).
  • Shared ground worth saying aloud: the TAS itself states that intermediaries are “not a dependency for trust between endpoints”.
  • Picture: Ana and Ben meet (phones, QR); the network grows from encounters, friend by friend, without any group yet. Groups come on slide 07.
  • The credential form is not where we differ: DTG WD 0.6.0, “two VRCs (one each direction) form a complete DTG edge”; First Person, VRCs “issued in pairs”. Encounter 0.32 does exactly this: one credential per confirmation, fresh challenge, an enactment binding anyone can recompute.
  • The fork is the anchor. In the DTG personhood hangs on the community-issued VMC (one membership per person); a First Person VRC “may expire or be revoked by the issuer”. Here nobody issued it: never revoked, never expires, no validUntil, no credentialStatus (Encounter 7); a change of mind is a new credential.
  • What it proves (Encounter 3): the key was in the right hands at that moment and a human decided. Not that they stood in the same room, not who they are, not that either trusts the other. “The protocol does not prove personhood.” RLTP-ENC-8020: a credential pair is never presented as proof that a meeting took place.
  • Price: reach. What two people know, no stranger learns by default; trust across strangers has to be built on purpose.

Compared with OpenVTC

  • There: the basic unit is the community (VTC): own DID, member list, policy engine, registry. A relationship credential counts as an edge only if the issuer’s membership is proven.
  • Here: the relationship is the unit; communities are bound to relationships, not the other way round.
  • Shared: relationship credentials in pairs, one per direction, and the in-person check with a spoken code.
  • There: membership, the personhood flag and endorsements are community-issued, with a status list; memberships expire (30 days by default). Here: no issuer, no status endpoint, no renewal.

04 · Trust

Trust is not a level. It is what you choose to show.

After an encounter, a contact sees the stars: who we both know and which groups we share, blinded. The Trust act gives that one contact the key that ties my relationships together, and with it who I am in our groups, my name and picture, and that they may introduce me. Nothing carries a degree. A class answers what, not how much.

Real Life Trust Protocol

  • Trust Layer 0.31, section 2a: a closed list of five classes (2720): the star (which of my contacts this contact also knows, blinded, verifiable only by the addressee and only for anchors it holds), the group star (which groups we share, blinded), the anchor mapping (the key that ties my relationships together, on toward nobody until the Trust act), the sealed group star (my member anchor in a group we share), the profile.
  • The introduction row is an outlook: in 0.31 introduction request and reply are class-D artifacts (8.2), not a trust class; 0.32 is drafted, not published.
  • Each class stands per contact: default, on or off. The Trust act turns the anchor mapping on (2770) and every class whose default is “trusted contacts” follows. Taking it back stops what followed the default; what was switched on by hand stays on.
  • Picture, three steps: Ben’s panel after the encounter (the two stars on by default), the Trust button, the same panel after the act.
  • 2750: no artifact of a trust class carries a degree of trust; a global score “is either forgeable or central”. 1010: an anchor reaches a party only through an act of its issuer toward that party; nobody forwards another person’s key.
  • The fork, in their own words: in the DTG, more than “I know this person” is a statement credential, dtg:endorses, “an endorsement is evidence … a VSC attests; it never establishes” (WD 0.6.0). First Person v1.2 calls vouching the step “from a trust graph to a reputation graph” and warns: “Here be dragons.” Here trust is an act toward one contact that changes what that contact sees; nothing travels further.

Compared with OpenVTC

  • There: trust in a person runs through the community: roles (admin, moderator, issuer, member), policy in Rego, endorsements as statement credentials.
  • Here: one act toward one contact that opens the link between my own identities to that contact alone; access per contact and class.
  • Shared: no score on either side.

05 · Audience

Knowing is not proving.

Before anything gets a proof, two questions: whom must it convince, and may the receiver show it on? Most things only need to convince one person, so only that person can check them. Cards and encounter credentials are signed, because their reader is someone I have not met. What ties two of my identities together is never signed. What I say about others is blinded.

Real Life Trust Protocol

  • Trust Layer 0.31, section 3. Two questions decide every artifact’s form: whom must it convince (F1), must the receiver be able to pass it on (F2). 3010: the verifier set is the smallest the purpose demands. 3020: deniable is the default; a transferable signature carries the burden of justification.
  • Four classes. S, about myself to one addressee (profile, anchor mapping): a MAC, only the addressee. P, presentable (cards, encounter credential, membership document): signed for anyone (3030). V, links between two of my contexts: deniable, and “MUST NOT exist anywhere as a transferable proof” (3040). D, statements about third parties (star, group star, continuity probe): deniable and blinded, the knowledge itself harms (3060).
  • The fork, precisely: the DTG protects privacy by hiding which credential is shown (“ZKP presentation the default behavior”) while the artifacts stay signed and transferable. Its own Privacy Considerations (WD 0.6.0, item 8): the membership acknowledgement “is a signed, transferable credential … the community can prove that membership to a third party without the member’s involvement”.
  • Here it is the other way round: deniable toward one addressee by default. Central commitment: links between anchors are the protected good. Trust travels with the person, through cards and introductions, never as a transferable proof about a relationship.

Compared with OpenVTC

  • There: the presentable form is the norm: credentials with proofs, status lists outside verifiers read without a login, a registry with a query protocol; ZKP presentation meant as default, today behind a feature flag.
  • Here: the deniable form is the norm; a transferable signature is the exception that needs a reason.

06 · Relational reading

Trust is read from where you stand.

Identities are free, so a swarm can vouch for itself. That is why "many people know me" counts only like this: at least N, of which k inside the trust of whoever is asking. Ben reads Ana's relationships differently from Sam, because they trust different people. There is no number everyone reads the same way. It would be forgeable, or central.

Real Life Trust Protocol

  • Trust Layer 0.31, section 7, and Personhood Predicates 0.12. Anchors are free, so a Sybil swarm issues itself any amount of self-evidence; the scarce thing is the edge into the honest graph.
  • Shared ground: DTG WD 0.6.0, “edge verifiability is a property of a credential with respect to a verifier, not of the credential alone”. Reading is verifier-relative on both sides.
  • The fork is the anchor: in the DTG the verifier evaluates against the communities it accepts; First Person proves “at least Y VRCs in a PHC ecosystem”, with Sybil resistance from the one-per-person rule, which the ZKP draft itself calls “governance, not cryptography”. Here the anchor is the reader’s own contacts.
  • 7010: a count or personhood statement is anchored relationally, “at least N, of which k inside the verifier’s trust horizon”, never absolutely. 7020: no layer accepts a global score. 7030, honest: the proof artifact for relational counts is unwritten; until then 7010 binds as a design constraint.
  • Picture: each person sees only their own star; laid on top, the shared contacts and groups light up; everything else stays dark. The blinded star is the instrument: it tells the reader which of Ana’s contacts they already hold themselves.
  • Personhood Predicates: three verifier-relative measures (anchors the verifier already knows, signed instants, asserters a group’s admission vouches for), and the limit in the same breath: “No predicate here says ‘this is a human’.”

Compared with OpenVTC

  • There: personhood is a flag on the membership credential, by its own documentation a non-binding hint and a governance decision; the registry says who is a member.
  • Here: personhood is read by each viewer from their own horizon.
  • Shared: neither accepts a global number.

07 · Invitation

Invitation, not checkpoint.

Nobody has to present anything to get in, not at an encounter and not into a group. Those who let you in know you. The devices only check that the invitation followed the group's rules and that you said yes yourself. No credential is produced that you would have to show somewhere else.

Real Life Trust Protocol

  • The admission question, before the group itself. Shared ground, stated fairly: the DTG also invites and also asks consent. WD 0.6.0 has the Verifiable Invitation Credential, and membership is a pair, the community’s grant plus the member’s acknowledgement, which “a community cannot produce without that party’s signature”.
  • The fork is who lets you in. VTI WD 0.2.0: “A VTC is its membership secretary. It decides who joins”; VTI-MEM-011, “Completion of a join exchange MUST NOT be treated as admission. Admission is the decision”; VTI-MEM-020, membership “MUST be evidenced by a credential issued by the community”.
  • Here there is no secretary (Membership Tasks 0.21.1, Access 0.58): invite, the invitee’s own signed accept, and member.add by any authorized member; the group’s policy decides whether the inviter was allowed to invite and how many members must vouch. The checks run on the devices of those involved; nothing portable results, no third party keeps a log.
  • Joining gives you a fresh key for that group. The members see that key in the signed member.add; only the inviter can tie it to the person they know, and nothing in the group says who you are elsewhere.
  • Picture: first the checkpoint (show, check, admit, a log that grows); then, in its place, the invitation (know, invite, share).
  • Honest footnote: the invitation payload is a conformant DTG InvitationCredential (RLTP-MT-3010), still pinned to DTG Working Draft 01 and its old context IRI; lifting it to WD 0.6.0 is open.
  • Price: someone who knows nobody cannot get in. Access for strangers is something a group builds on purpose, with presentable cards (slide 05).

Compared with OpenVTC

  • There: joining is a checkpoint: a policy (default deny all) plus admin approval, even for open requests; invitation credentials replace the waiting, not the check. “Two vouchers from the membership” is written down, not active.
  • Here: members invite; the devices only check the rule.

08 · Group

No one stands above the group’s own record.

A group is a signed record every member holds, and its rules are part of it: who may invite, who may remove, how the rules change. People join by invitation and their own consent (slide 08). No role stands above the record. When the founder leaves, the group goes on.

Real Life Trust Protocol

  • Who holds the truth about a group, and what happens when they are gone? VTI-VTC-001: a community “MUST be provisioned on an existing VTA”; it “decides who joins, records who is in good standing”; roles come as authority credentials. First Person v1.2 expects communities “managed by multiple administrators”.
  • Here the truth is one record, held by every member. Access 0.58: the authority log is “a causally linked DAG of individually signed operations rooted in a single-founder genesis whose digest IS the group’s identity”; state is its deterministic materialization; policies are group-defined decision rules; epochs make removal real.
  • README: “There are no admins. Privileged operations are gated by a decision rule the group states as data; a single founder is the k = 1 special case. Contested authority never picks a winner: the group fails closed, visibly, until a quorum of both branches reconciles.”
  • A group may still give one person the say; the difference to an administrator outside is that this person is a member inside the record. Picture: Ana, the founder, leaves; the group admits Lea afterwards.
  • Price: complexity. Concurrent changes need fixed rules for every pairing (the conflict matrix, Access 3.6), and there is no kill switch when a group goes wrong. The record keeps decisions; it does not make them.
  • Status (Access, Status of This Document): the library implements wire types, schemas and derivations; the authority rules, the conflict matrix and the two ports run against the beekem/0.1 adapter in an experimental harness, not yet in the library.

Compared with OpenVTC

  • There: a community is a server with its own database and cached signing keys; its access list is authoritative; M-of-N governance designed, not built. Data rooms have one owner by invariant: room DID, all credentials, sole MLS committer.
  • Here: every member holds the record; the rules are data inside it.

  • README: “There are no admins. Privileged operations are gated by a decision rule the group states as data; a single founder is the k = 1 special case. Contested authority never picks a winner: the group fails closed, visibly, until a quorum of both branches reconciles.”

09 · Offline

A neighborhood keeps deciding when the network is gone.

The group lives on its members' devices, and every copy may change. Two people who meet can carry on alone; what they decided apart is merged by the group's own rules when the others return. Relays, mesh or a phone next to yours only carry sealed bytes. No host has to be awake.

Real Life Trust Protocol

  • Shared ground, so nobody has to defend the web: ToIP TAS PR02, “Internet support is not strictly required”; VTI-TRN-020, a mediator “MUST NOT be able to read the content it forwards”; “a dead mediator is not a dead peer”.
  • The fork is the community node: in VTI the community is a service on an agent, and whether a claim is still true is “a live query to the community’s trust registry”. Here the group lives on its members’ devices; every copy may change, and the conflict matrix of the previous slide decides what concurrent changes mean.
  • Replication Contract 0.26: sixteen promises over individually signed, causally linked entries; the substrate that moves the bytes stays outside the trusted computing base; no transport and no CRDT named (the app’s substrate may use one). Delivery Contract 0.85.2 asks a carrier for one promise: “eventually, at least once, never silently lost”; delivery time is open, a carrier pigeon qualifies.
  • A carrier sees one address per relationship and carrier (Identity 7a), never a person, so relationships do not converge at whoever carries them.
  • Price: conflicts become visible instead of prevented; two truths may stand side by side for a while.
  • Honest: the repository evidences the encounter’s offline fallback in the simulator and in the app (the credential is issued without a mediator; delivery waits), not a run of a whole group without any network between neighbours’ devices. Open: exactly that run.
  • Question for the room: for which communities is the hour without a host an edge case, and for which is it daily life?

Compared with OpenVTC

  • There: a mediator with store-and-forward, so the recipient need not be online at the same time; but agent, DID host and mediator must be reachable. Data rooms reject CRDTs in favour of one write primary.
  • Here: concurrent offline writing is the normal case; the group’s own rules merge it.
  • Honest: our own run with no network at all has barely happened.

10 · On the wire

On the wire, we speak the ecosystem's forms.

The principles are ours. The forms are shared: DTG credentials, Trust Task documents, TSP through a mediator, did:key identities. A second implementation can read what we send, and we can read theirs. That is what a build weekend can test.

Real Life Trust Protocol

  • The merge slide, checked against the public repository on 28 October 2026.
  • Encounter credential: W3C VC 2.0 of type VerifiableCredential, DTGCredential, RelationshipCredential, EncounterCredential, DTG context https://registry.trustoverip.org/dtg/context/v1 as second context (RLTP-ENC-7010, 2260); the conformance runner also reads a plain foreign DTG VRC. Verified both ways with Consensual on 11 October: their independent verifier confirmed ours, our reader their twelve dyad VRCs under did:peer:2 (trust-protocol #74); recorded on the DTG interop board.
  • Invitation: a conformant DTG InvitationCredential inside membership-invite (Membership Tasks 0.21.1, RLTP-MT-3010), pinned to DTG Working Draft 01; lifting it to WD 0.6.0 is open.
  • Messages: Trust Task documents declared on framework 0.6 (type URIs with /spec/); the framework moved to WD 0.7.0 on 28 October, one step pending.
  • Transport: VTI mediator adapter 0.30 binds TSP 1.0 and nothing else, routed through the VTI mediator (own instance at mediator.web-of-trust.de), with the registry’s TSP binding 0.1 and trust-task-error 0.6. Live probes run only with RLTP_LIVE_MEDIATOR=1 against the OpenVTC development mediator; routed delivery measured 12 of 12 (E11b, port-experimente #76, #81, #82, merged 21 October). TSP 1.0 “provides no delivery guarantee”, so the adapter is a transport contract, not the full carrier port.
  • Identities: did:key, one anchor per context from one seed (Identity 0.55); VTI itself requires did:key for a client’s bootstrap identity (VTI-CLT-003).
  • Not used: r-cards (RFC 9553 JSContact) for profiles; an idea, not built.

Compared with OpenVTC

  • Where we meet: DTG credentials, Trust Tasks, TSP 1.0 through the same mediator, did:key.
  • Open: the Trust Task framework version gap (0.6 against 0.7), and whether our encounter credentials count as edges in their graph.

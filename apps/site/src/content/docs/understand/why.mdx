Digital trust is built around one of two figures: the checkpoint or the
invitation. Most work starts at the checkpoint. A person presents a
credential at a door, someone they have never met checks it, and the
door opens or stays shut. RLTP is built around the invitation. It starts
with what a person wants from their own network.

<blockquote class="tp-quote">
  <p>“I want my own graph. To see how I'm connected to anyone I meet. To watch it grow. To build things with the people in it.”</p>
  <p class="who">Anton Tranelis</p>
</blockquote>

Showing proof does not come up in that wish, because the graph is alive,
a field you tend rather than an archive you carry. Neither figure is
wrong. Each answers a different problem.

## The checkpoint

A person carries credentials issued by institutions and presents them at
a door. A stranger checks them against a policy and admits the person or
not. Every passage is checked again, and the log grows.

<figure class="tp-half"><CheckpointHalf /></figure>

The checkpoint comes from self-sovereign identity, which began with an
institutional problem: a person has to prove something to an
organisation at a distance, without a platform becoming the gatekeeper.
Bank checks, diplomas, driving licences, proof of age. The other side is
a stranger by definition. No relationship, and none wanted; proof is the
only currency. Issuer, holder, verifier: the roles of the paper ID card.
Presenting was never a design choice. It was inherited.

## The invitation

Nobody presents anything. You consult your own graph: where do we know
each other from, what do we share? A friend, a group, an event, a place.
Then you invite into a shared place. The decision stays with you and
becomes a relationship. The graph itself grows out of
[real encounters](/understand/encounter/): two people meet, and each
confirms that they recognize the other.

<figure class="tp-half"><InvitationHalf /></figure>

The invitation comes from community organising and local-first
software: maps, camps, festivals, regional networks. That is where we
come from. From the start we wanted to work in groups of people we know
from real life, and to build things with them. Trust
comes first. It begins when people meet. Software can remember it and
carry it. It cannot create it. We never had to learn to present, because
people who know each other don't need to.

## What makes a checkpoint

Both figures contain a moment of checking. At the checkpoint, a
stranger checks your papers. At the invitation, you check your own
graph. Checking turns into a checkpoint when three things meet: a
stranger decides, the proof travels, and a third party keeps the log.
RLTP checks too: when two people meet, when someone joins a group, when
a document arrives. But the host decides, what remains is a relationship
rather than a proof to carry elsewhere, and nobody outside keeps a log.

## Who is the trust statement for?

Underneath the two figures lies one question: who is the trust
statement for? A statement meant for strangers needs an issuer they
accept and infrastructure they can reach. Trust that comes from your
own relationships needs neither. That is why the invitation works
offline, on a festival field or in a village without a connection: no
server and no authority stands in the path.

A credential freezes trust. A relationship keeps it alive. A frozen
object can be checked by someone who knows nothing else about you; a
living relationship needs people who know each other. So each figure
works where the other fails. The invitation fails between strangers,
and deliberately so: a real encounter is our answer to fake identities.
Border crossings, proof of age and admissions among strangers need
checkpoints. The checkpoint fails inside a community: a community that
checks its own members' credentials has stopped being one. Checkpoints
belong at the edges of social space; invitations belong inside it.

## Membership isn't a proof, it's a key

Decentralized identifiers and verifiable credentials have five building
blocks: the identifier, the credential, the presentation, the status and
the wallet. Each is a statement about someone, held by someone, shown to
someone. A group appears only as an issuer of membership credentials, as
certificates scattered across wallets, or as a database. To learn
whether someone belongs, you ask the database or have something shown.

RLTP adds a sixth building block:
[the group as an object of its own](/understand/foundations/#groups). It
is an encrypted document on every member's device, with a log of who
joined, who left and which rules apply. Whoever is inside holds the key,
and the key opens the shared data. Being a member means holding a place,
not holding a claim.

| Statement | Place |
|---|---|
| Signed, portable, presented. Grants nothing by itself; a verifier grants access each time. | Shared, encrypted, governed by its own rules. You are invited in, and inside you hold the key. |
| The grammar of institutions: ID card, certificate, contract. | The grammar of communities: room, circle, table. |

A checkpoint is what you build when there is no shared place; an
invitation needs one. We did not invent the parts of that place.
Replicated data, group keys and event logs come from local-first
software. What is new is binding them to trust.

## Where the two worlds meet

The two worlds meet, and statements cross in both directions. A
credential from the institutional world can become an edge in
someone's graph. An encounter credential from ours can serve as
evidence where a stranger needs it. What does not cross is the grammar
of checking: no registry, no duty to present, no log of who was
checked. And a place never turns into a statement. You can show that
you met someone; you cannot hand someone a group.

The protocol keeps to that rule, from its carriers to its credentials;
[Foundations](/understand/foundations/) describes how. Where regulation
demands presenting, as with proof of age, we build windows, not master
keys: a narrow proof answers exactly one question, and the graph itself
is never shown.

One side optimizes showing. We optimize inviting. Same credential, two
verbs.
